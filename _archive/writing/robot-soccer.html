Robot learning

Teaching a robot dog to take a shot

One reinforcement-learning policy that walks a Unitree Go1 to a ball, kicks it at a target, and holds still.

Amal Mehta · October 9, 2026 · 2 min read

Kicking a ball sounds like one skill. It's really three in a row: get to the ball, kick it the right way, and don't fall over afterwards.

At Berkeley's Hybrid Robotics Lab we worked on a Unitree Go1, a small four-legged robot, learning to shoot a soccer ball at a target. Earlier work in the lab broke this into separate policies, one per skill, with the kick following a planned curve. We wanted to see if a single policy could learn the whole thing.

One policy, three stages

The policy is trained with PPO in IsaacGym, a GPU simulator that runs thousands of robots at once. The trick is telling it which part of the task it's in.

A status flag does that. Two distances drive it: how far the robot is from the ball, and how far the ball has moved from where it started. Far from the ball means stage one. Close, with the ball still sitting there, means kick. Once the ball has moved, it's stage three, whatever else is true.

Each stage gets its own rewards. Stage one rewards following a reference trot, closing the distance, moving at the right speed and facing the ball. Stage two stops the legs, then rewards sending the ball in the target's direction while keeping the posture steady. The reward weights rise from stage to stage, so the policy keeps wanting to move forward through the task.

One small problem took real thought. In stage one, the ball's live position and the target don't matter yet, but the network needs the same-sized input in every stage. So those slots hold placeholder values until they matter. The input shape never changes, and the policy moves between stages smoothly.

On the real robot

The policy trained in simulation and ran on the real Go1. It hit the target about 60% of the time.

That's not perfect, and that's the interesting part. Simulation gets the robot most of the way there. The last stretch is the gap between the simulated world and the real one, all the physics a simulator gets slightly wrong. My part was the stage-switching reward machinery and the gait tracking, and I prototyped RLPD, a way to fine-tune the simulated policy on real robot data, aimed at exactly that gap.

That gap is the thread through most of what I work on now.
